Barrier to Thermally Activated Glide Past Obstacles

How the energy barrier that a dislocation must overcome falls as the applied stress rises, for five obstacle shapes and for a short-range or a long-range obstacle, with a fit of the Kocks–Argon–Ashby barrier equation.

Instructions for use
  • Obstacle shape: choose Gaussian, sinusoidal, hyperbolic, triangular or parabolic. Changing the shape clears the lower plot and refits the equation.
  • τ slider: sets the applied stress (dimensionless, 0 to 1). The top plot shows the free energy of the dislocation as a function of its position x for the two obstacles; the dots mark the local minimum and the local maximum, the light dotted lines show the energy profile at τ = 0 for reference, and the table beside it gives their positions and the barrier ΔG.
  • Start / Pause: steps τ automatically from 0 to 1 and then restarts. Clear points erases the lower plot. Plot whole curve fills in the barrier for every τ from 0 to 1 at once.
  • Lower plot: every time τ changes, the barrier ΔG (the free-energy difference between the maximum and the minimum) is added as a point. Tick Show Kocks fit (off by default) to draw the fitted Kocks–Argon–Ashby equation as a dashed line and compare it with the points.
  • Fit table (beside the lower plot) gives the fitted ΔG0, p and q for each obstacle, and how well the equation reproduces the exact barrier.
  • Blue: short-range and weak obstacle (SRW). Red: long-range and small-force obstacle (LRS). Both have the same shape; the SRW obstacle is 0.2 times the LRS obstacle in height and in width.

Physics of the problem

A dislocation gliding through a crystal meets obstacles (solute atoms, precipitates, other dislocations). Its free energy as a function of position is the energy of the obstacle plus the work done by the applied stress as the dislocation advances through a distance \(x\):

\( G(x) = G_0(x) - \tau\,x \)

Here \(G_0(x)\) is the energy hill of the obstacle, of height \(A\) and half-width \(w\). At \(\tau = 0\) the dislocation must climb the full height of the hill, \(\Delta G = A\). As \(\tau\) rises, the tilt \(-\tau x\) creates a minimum in front of the obstacle and a maximum close to its top, and the barrier

\( \Delta G(\tau) = G(x_\text{max}) - G(x_\text{min}) \)

shrinks. The extrema are the roots of \(dG/dx = 0\), that is of \(G_0'(x) = \tau\). The minimum lies on the rising front of the hill, where \(G_0'' \gt 0\), and the maximum on the concave part near the top. The barrier vanishes at the stress at which the two roots merge, the athermal (mechanical-threshold) stress \(\tau_c = \max\,|G_0'|\).

The five obstacle shapes

An obstacle can be described by its energy–displacement curve \(G_0(x)\) or by its force–displacement curve \(F(x)=G_0'(x)\), the two being related by integration; the literature uses both. The tool shows both: the free-energy plot at the top and the force plot below it, in which the applied stress \(\tau\) is a horizontal line and the extrema of \(G\) are where that line cuts the force curve. The names below are those of the energy hill, with the force profile in brackets.

  • Gaussian: \(G_0 = A\,e^{-(x/w)^2}\). Smooth, with long tails (force: a smooth bump with long tails).
  • Sinusoidal: \(G_0 = \tfrac{A}{2}\,[1+\cos(\pi x/w)]\) for \(|x| \lt w\), and zero outside. Smooth, with finite range (force: a half-sine arch). It has a closed-form barrier, with \(s=\tau/\tau_c\):
    \( \Delta G = A\left[\sqrt{1-s^2} - s\,\arccos s\right] \)
  • Hyperbolic: \(G_0 = A\,\operatorname{sech}^2(x/w)\). The profile of a soliton, with exponential tails, between the Gaussian and the sinusoid in sharpness (force: sech² tanh).
  • Triangular (rectangular force profile): Kocks, Argon and Ashby describe an obstacle by its force–displacement profile, and the rectangular profile is a constant resisting force \(F=A/w\) acting over the distance \(w\) in front of the obstacle. Integrating the force gives an energy hill that is a triangle, \(G_0 = A\,(1-|x|/w)\), and the barrier is exactly linear, \(\Delta G = A\,(1-\tau/\tau_c)\), with \(\tau_c = A/w\). (A rectangular energy profile, a box of height \(A\), would give \(\Delta G=A\) at every stress below the box force and has no extrema to find.)
  • Parabolic (triangular force profile): the resisting force rises linearly from zero to its peak \(\hat F = 2A/w\) at \(x=-w/2\) and falls linearly to zero at the centre of the obstacle, and the same on the far side. Integrating gives an energy hill made of parabolic arcs: \(G_0 = 2A\,(1+x/w)^2\) for \(-w \lt x \lt -w/2\) and \(G_0 = A\,(1-2x^2/w^2)\) for \(-w/2 \lt x \lt 0\), mirrored for \(x \gt 0\). The force has a sharp (cusp-like) peak, so the two extrema merge linearly rather than as a fold, and the barrier is exactly \(\Delta G = A\,(1-\tau/\tau_c)^2\), with \(\tau_c = 2A/w\). This is \(p=1\), \(q=2\).

The widths are scaled as \(w = \kappa A/\tau_c^{*}\), with \(\tau_c^{*}=\sqrt{2}\,e^{-1/2}\approx 0.858\) and \(\kappa = \sqrt{2}\,e^{-1/2}\), \(\pi/2\), \(4/(3\sqrt{3})\), 1 and 2 for the five shapes. With this choice the Gaussian is the same as before (\(w=0.2\) and \(w=1\)) and all shapes have the same athermal stress, so the shapes are compared at equal \(\tau_c\).

Short-range and long-range obstacles

The short-range weak obstacle has \(A=0.2\) and the long-range one \(A=1\), with the widths scaled in the same ratio. The two hills therefore have the same shape, and \(G_\text{SRW}(x,\tau) = 0.2\,G_\text{LRS}(x/0.2,\tau)\). It follows that \(\Delta G_\text{SRW}(\tau)=0.2\,\Delta G_\text{LRS}(\tau)\) exactly: the two obstacles have the same \(\tau_c\), and the same normalised curve \(\Delta G/A\). They differ in the energy scale and in the distance over which the dislocation moves, and hence in the activation volume \(V^*=-\partial \Delta G/\partial\tau\), which is five times larger for the LRS obstacle.

The Kocks–Argon–Ashby equation

The barrier is commonly represented by

\( \Delta G = \Delta G_0\left[1-\left(\frac{\tau}{\hat\tau}\right)^{p}\right]^{q} \)

with \(\Delta G_0\) the barrier at zero stress, \(\hat\tau\) the athermal stress, and the exponents bounded by \(0 \lt p\le 1\) and \(1\le q\le 2\) for physically reasonable obstacles. The tool fits \(\Delta G_0\), \(p\) and \(q\) to the exact barrier at 200 stresses between 0 and \(0.999\,\tau_c\) by least squares (Nelder–Mead, from several starting values), with \(\hat\tau=\tau_c\) held fixed. Two checks are useful. For the triangular obstacle (rectangular force profile) the fit returns \(p=q=1\) with no error, and for the parabolic obstacle (triangular force profile) it returns \(p=1\), \(q=2\) with no error; these two bracket the range of \(q\). For any smooth hill, the two extrema merge as a fold, so that \(\Delta G\propto(1-\tau/\tau_c)^{3/2}\) close to \(\tau_c\), and a free fit tends to a value of \(q\) near 3/2 for the Gaussian, sinusoidal and hyperbolic shapes. A cusp-like force peak gives \(q=2\), and a force that is constant (a rectangle) gives \(q=1\). Because the fit is least squares over the whole range, \(p\) and \(q\) are correlated, and different pairs can describe the curve almost equally well.

At a finite temperature the dislocation can pass the obstacle before \(\tau_c\) is reached with the help of thermal activation, at a rate proportional to \(\exp(-\Delta G/kT)\). The shape of \(\Delta G(\tau)\) therefore determines the temperature and strain-rate sensitivity of the flow stress, and the activation volume.

Glossary
τ
Applied shear stress, dimensionless.
ΔG
Activation free energy, the barrier between the local minimum and the local maximum of G(x).
SRW
Short-range, weak obstacle: narrow and low.
LRS
Long-range obstacle: wide and tall.
Athermal stress, τc
The stress at which the barrier vanishes and no thermal help is needed (written τ̂ in the fitted equation).
ΔG0
The fitted barrier at zero stress. For a good fit it is close to the height A of the obstacle.
p, q
Exponents of the Kocks–Argon–Ashby equation, which set the shape of the ΔG–τ curve. p = q = 1 is a straight line.
Rectangular force profile
An obstacle that exerts a constant force over a fixed distance; its energy hill is a triangle, which is why the shape is called triangular here.
Force–displacement curve
The resisting force of the obstacle, \(F=dG_0/dx\), against position; the energy curve is its integral.
Activation volume
The (negative) slope of the barrier with respect to stress.
