Constitutive Equations: True and Engineering Stress–Strain Curves

Three models of work hardening (Hollomon, Ludwik, Voce), and how a true stress–true strain curve converts into engineering stress–strain in tension and in compression.

Instructions for use
  • Choose a model: click the circle next to Hollomon, Ludwik or Voce, then edit its parameters. The plot updates as you type.
  • Plot settings: Max true strain is the end of the curve, Points is the number of points used to draw it, and Sample points is how many marked points (P1, P2, …) are placed along it.
  • Tension and compression boxes: add the engineering stress–strain curves (blue dashed for tension, red dotted for compression). Arrows lead from each sample point on the true curve to its engineering counterpart.
  • Table box: lists the numerical values at the sample points.
  • The axes rescale so that every curve shown stays in view.

Model and parameters

\( \sigma = K\varepsilon^n \)
\( \sigma = \sigma_0 + K\varepsilon^n \)
\( \sigma = \sigma_s - (\sigma_s-\sigma_0)\,e^{-\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0} \)
Physics of the problem

When a metal is deformed plastically it work-hardens: the flow stress rises with accumulated strain, because dislocations multiply and obstruct one another. The true stress \(\sigma = F/A\) and the true strain \(\varepsilon = \ln(L/L_0)\) use the instantaneous area and length. Three empirical laws describe the hardening:

  • Hollomon: \(\sigma = K\varepsilon^n\). Simple, with a strain-hardening exponent \(n\) of about 0.1 to 0.5, but it gives zero stress at zero strain and so is a poor description of the early stages.
  • Ludwik: \(\sigma = \sigma_0 + K\varepsilon^n\). It adds a yield stress \(\sigma_0\).
  • Voce: \(\sigma = \sigma_s-(\sigma_s-\sigma_0)e^{-\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0}\). The hardening rate falls steadily and the stress saturates at \(\sigma_s\), as when dynamic recovery balances dislocation storage.

The engineering quantities use the original area \(A_0\) and length \(L_0\): \(s=F/A_0\) and \(e=(L-L_0)/L_0\). If the volume is constant and the deformation is uniform:

Tension: \( e = e^{\varepsilon}-1,\quad s = \sigma/(1+e) = \sigma e^{-\varepsilon} \)
Compression: \( e = 1-e^{-\varepsilon},\quad s = \sigma\,e^{\varepsilon} \) (magnitudes)

In tension the area shrinks, so the engineering stress falls below the true stress and can even pass through a maximum, the ultimate tensile strength. In compression the area grows, so the engineering stress is above the true stress. The two engineering curves differ for the same material, but the true curve is the same. This is why constitutive laws are written in true quantities.

The conversion holds only as long as the strain is uniform. In a tension test it ceases to be valid at necking, which begins when the hardening rate falls to the level of the stress (Considère criterion, \(d\sigma/d\varepsilon=\sigma\)). For the Hollomon law this happens at \(\varepsilon = n\).

Glossary
True stress, σ
Force divided by the instantaneous cross-sectional area.
True strain, ε
The natural logarithm of the ratio of current length to original length.
Engineering stress, s
Force divided by the original cross-sectional area.
Engineering strain, e
Change in length divided by original length.
K, n
Strength coefficient and strain-hardening exponent.
σ0, σs
Initial (yield) stress and saturation stress.
Necking
Localisation of tensile deformation into a small region, after which the stress and strain are no longer uniform.
