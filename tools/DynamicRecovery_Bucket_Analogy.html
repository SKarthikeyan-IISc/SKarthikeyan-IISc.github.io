Dynamic Recovery: Dislocation Density as Water in a Leaking Bucket

A bucket filled by a tap at a fixed rate, with a small hole in its bottom, as an analogy for how the dislocation density evolves when dislocations are produced at a steady rate and removed by dynamic recovery.

Instructions for use
  • Set the inlet and the outlet. Inflow is the rate at which the tap fills the bucket (the production rate of dislocations). Hole diameter sets the size of the leak at the bottom (the recovery). The bucket diameter and the starting water level are also adjustable. Any change to a setting stops the run and resets it.
  • Setting the inflow to zero switches the tap off. The bucket then only drains, which is the analogue of static recovery (see the physics note).
  • Press Start. The simulation then runs by itself: the water level in the bucket is the dislocation density. Pause and Start again to continue, or press Reset to go back to the starting level.
  • Playback speed scales simulated time against real time. It is set automatically so that a run lasts about 12 s, and you can change it. The clock under the bucket always shows simulated time.
  • Top plot: water level against time. The solid line is the simulation; the dashed white line is the exact solution of the equation, and the dashed green line is the steady level the bucket must reach. A red dashed line marks the rim of the bucket, which appears only if the bucket would overflow.
  • Bottom plot: the inflow (production) and the outflow (recovery) rates. The level stops changing when the two are equal.
  • Try a smaller hole at the same inflow: the steady level rises, and so does the time to reach it. A very small hole makes the bucket overflow, the analogue of a material with so little recovery that the density keeps rising (stage II hardening).

Physics of the problem

During plastic flow, dislocations are stored in the crystal and so the dislocation density \(\rho\) rises, which is strain hardening. At the same time dynamic recovery, the annihilation of dislocations by cross-slip and climb, removes them at a rate that grows with \(\rho\). The density therefore tends to a saturation value, at which production and removal balance, and the flow stress saturates with it. A bucket that is filled at a fixed rate and drains through a small hole in its bottom behaves in the same way. The mapping is:

BucketCrystal
Water level \(h\)Dislocation density \(\rho\)
Inflow from the tap, \(Q_\text{in}\)Production (storage) rate of dislocations
Outflow through the hole, \(Q_\text{out}\)Dynamic recovery (annihilation) rate
Steady level \(h^*\)Saturation density, hence saturation stress
Time \(t\)Strain (at a constant strain rate)

What the two controls stand for. Changing the inflow is like changing the dislocation production rate. Changing the size of the hole is like changing the recovery kinetics. Recovery by cross-slip and climb is thermally activated, so a larger hole corresponds to a higher temperature, and a smaller hole to a lower one. At a higher temperature the density therefore saturates at a lower value, and the flow stress with it. Setting the inflow to its minimum, zero, is analogous to static recovery: nothing is being produced, as when a deformed crystal is held at temperature without further straining, and the stored density falls by recovery alone. The bucket then simply drains.

The equations used by the simulation are as follows.

1. Conservation of volume. Water is incompressible, so the volume in the bucket, \(A_b h\), changes by what comes in minus what goes out. With \(A_b = \pi D^2/4\) the area of the bucket,

\( A_b\,\dfrac{dh}{dt} = Q_\text{in} - Q_\text{out} \)

2. Outflow through the hole: Torricelli's law. Bernoulli's equation between the free surface and the jet leaving the hole gives a jet speed \(v=\sqrt{2gh}\) (the surface speed is negligible because the hole is much smaller than the bucket, \(d \ll D\)). The jet contracts just outside a sharp-edged hole, which is allowed for by a discharge coefficient \(C_d \approx 0.61\):

\( Q_\text{out} = C_d\,a\,\sqrt{2gh}, \qquad a = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4} \)

The outflow is thus proportional to \(\sqrt{h}\), and not to \(h\). The law holds when the level changes slowly, so that the flow is quasi-steady, and when viscosity and surface tension can be ignored, as for a hole of a few mm or more in water.

3. Equation of the level. Substituting 2 in 1,

\( \dfrac{dh}{dt} = q - k\sqrt{h}, \qquad q = \dfrac{Q_\text{in}}{A_b}, \quad k = \dfrac{C_d\,a\,\sqrt{2g}}{A_b} \)

The simulation integrates this with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. If the water reaches the rim, \(h\) is held at the rim height and the excess spills over.

4. Steady level. The level stops changing when \(Q_\text{out} = Q_\text{in}\):

\( h^* = \dfrac{1}{2g}\left(\dfrac{Q_\text{in}}{C_d\,a}\right)^{2} \)

The steady level rises as the square of the inflow and falls as the fourth power of the hole diameter, since \(a \propto d^2\).

5. Exact solution. Put \(u=\sqrt{h}\), so that \(u^*=\sqrt{h^*}=q/k\) and \(2u\,du/dt = q - ku\). Integrating from the starting level \(u_0=\sqrt{h_0}\) gives \(t\) as a function of the level (the dashed line in the plot):

\( t = \dfrac{2}{k}\left[\,(u_0 - u) + u^*\ln\dfrac{u^*-u_0}{u^*-u}\,\right] \)

6. Approach to the steady level. For a small departure \(\delta h\) from \(h^*\), the equation becomes \(d(\delta h)/dt = -(k/2\sqrt{h^*})\,\delta h\), so the level approaches \(h^*\) exponentially, with the time constant

\( \tau = \dfrac{2\sqrt{h^*}}{k} = \dfrac{2A_b h^*}{Q_\text{in}} \)

This is twice the time that the tap would need to fill the steady volume. The level is within 1% of \(h^*\) after about \(5\tau\), and this is how long the plot runs. A higher inflow or a larger bucket makes \(\tau\) longer; hardening and recovery likewise set the strain over which the density saturates.

No inflow (static recovery). With \(Q_\text{in}=0\) the equation reduces to \(dh/dt = -k\sqrt{h}\), whose solution is \(\sqrt{h} = \sqrt{h_0} - kt/2\). The level falls to zero at \(t = 2\sqrt{h_0}/k\), and not exponentially, because of the \(\sqrt{h}\) law of the hole. A recovery term proportional to \(\rho\) would instead make the density decay exponentially and never reach zero.

A caution about the analogy. The recovery term in the usual model of dynamic recovery (Kocks–Mecking, \(d\rho/d\varepsilon = k_1\sqrt{\rho} - k_2\rho\)) is proportional to \(\rho\), whereas a hole gives an outflow proportional to \(\sqrt{h}\). The picture is right in kind: a constant source, and a loss that grows with the stored amount until the two balance. A leak with outflow proportional to \(h\) (laminar flow through a long, fine tube) would match the linear term exactly, and would give a pure exponential approach \(h = h^*(1-e^{-t/\tau})\). With the hole, the approach to saturation is exponential only close to \(h^*\).

Glossary
Dislocation density, ρ
Total length of dislocation line per unit volume of crystal.
Dynamic recovery
Annihilation and rearrangement of dislocations during deformation, by cross-slip and climb, which limits the build-up of density.
Production (storage) rate
The rate at which new dislocation line is stored per unit time or strain. Here, the inflow.
Static recovery
Recovery of a deformed crystal while it is held at temperature with no further straining, so that no new dislocations are produced. Here, the bucket with the tap turned off.
Saturation
The state in which production and recovery are equal, so that the density and the flow stress stop changing. Here, the steady level h*.
Torricelli's law
The speed of a jet from a small hole, v = √(2gh), set by the height of liquid above it.
Discharge coefficient, Cd
The ratio of the real flow through a hole to the ideal flow, which is less than one because the jet contracts. About 0.61 for a sharp-edged hole.
Time constant, τ
The time scale of the final approach to the steady level. The gap to h* shrinks by a factor of e in every τ.
