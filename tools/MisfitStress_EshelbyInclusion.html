Misfit Stress around a Spherical Precipitate

The elastic stress field of a spherical precipitate or solute atom in an isotropic matrix, found with Eshelby's equivalent-inclusion method. Set the size, the lattice misfit, the elastic constants of both phases and a remote applied stress, and probe the stress on a plane through the centre.

Instructions for use
  • Examples: the buttons at the top of the controls load typical cases, such as a coherent precipitate, a solute atom, and a hard particle or a pore under tension. Every control can then be changed.
  • Geometry and misfit: the radius r0 (0.1 to 30 nm) and the lattice misfit δ = (ap − am) / am, positive when the precipitate is oversized. The field of view can be set by hand or locked to 4r0.
  • Matrix and precipitate: the shear modulus and Poisson’s ratio of the matrix, and either the same constants for the precipitate or its own shear and bulk moduli. Set both moduli of the precipitate near zero to model a pore.
  • Applied stress: a remote stress (uniaxial along x or y, equibiaxial, pure shear or hydrostatic), positive in tension. With zero applied stress only the misfit field is shown.
  • Display (above the map): choose the stress component. Total field includes the applied stress; Disturbance only subtracts it and shows what the precipitate adds. The colour scale follows the largest value in view, or can be fixed so that two cases are compared on the same scale.
  • Map and probe: the map is the plane z = 0 through the centre of the sphere, with ten contour bands, blue for compression and red for tension for the signed components. Hover or drag on it to read all components at a point; the vertical line on the profile marks the same distance.
  • Profile and numbers: the profile shows the stresses along the x axis, and the panels under it give the constrained misfit strain, the pressure inside, the largest stress in view and the misfit energy, with the quantities used in the calculation.

Physics of the problem

The misfitting precipitate

A coherent precipitate, or a solute atom, whose lattice parameter differs from that of the matrix by \(\delta=(a_p-a_m)/a_m\) cannot fit into the hole it occupies. To keep the interface coherent, the precipitate is compressed and the matrix is stretched around it (for \(\delta>0\)), and both are elastically strained. The tool treats a sphere of radius \(r_0\) in an infinite, isotropic matrix of shear modulus \(\mu_m\) and Poisson’s ratio \(\nu_m\), and shows the stress on the plane \(z=0\) through its centre.

Eshelby’s equivalent inclusion

Eshelby (1957) showed that for an ellipsoid the strain inside is uniform, and that a particle with different elastic constants can be replaced by a particle of matrix material that carries a fictitious misfit strain, an eigenstrain \(\varepsilon^*\), chosen so that the stress inside is the same as in the real particle. For a sphere the Eshelby tensor has two numbers, set by the matrix only:

\( \alpha = \dfrac{1+\nu_m}{3(1-\nu_m)}, \qquad \beta = \dfrac{2(4-5\nu_m)}{15(1-\nu_m)} \)

The eigenstrain has a volumetric part, driven by the misfit and by any applied pressure, and a deviatoric part, driven only by an applied shear and only when \(\mu_p\neq\mu_m\). The strain inside the particle is then uniform, and so is the stress. The stress outside is the applied stress plus a disturbance that is the field of the eigenstrain.

Misfit alone

With no applied stress, the misfit produces a uniform hydrostatic pressure in the particle and a pure-shear field in the matrix:

\( p_\text{in} = 4\mu_m\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon = \dfrac{3K_p\,\delta}{3K_p+4\mu_m} \)
\( \sigma_{rr} = -4\mu_m\,\varepsilon\,(r_0/r)^3, \qquad \sigma_{\theta\theta}=\sigma_{\phi\phi} = +2\mu_m\,\varepsilon\,(r_0/r)^3 \)

The mean stress \(\sigma_{rr}+2\sigma_{\theta\theta}\) is zero everywhere in the matrix, so the misfit of a sphere produces no hydrostatic pressure outside it, only shear. This is why a spherical solute atom interacts with a screw dislocation only through second-order effects, and with an edge dislocation, whose field has a hydrostatic part, to first order. The field falls off as \(r^{-3}\), so it is short-ranged. The constrained strain \(\varepsilon\) is smaller than \(\delta\), and for equal elastic constants \(\varepsilon=\alpha\delta\).

Applied stress

An applied stress is concentrated by a stiffer or softer particle. A pore under tension is the extreme case: for uniaxial tension \(\sigma\) the stress at the equator of a spherical pore is \(\sigma\,(27-15\nu)/[2(7-5\nu)]\), about 2.05\(\sigma\) for \(\nu=0.3\). A hard particle carries more stress than the matrix and unloads it nearby. The \(r^{-3}\) and \(r^{-5}\) terms of the disturbance are visible in the profile, where the \(r^{-5}\) term matters near the interface.

Energy

The elastic energy stored by the misfit alone is

\( W = 6\,\mu_m\,\delta\,\varepsilon\,V, \qquad V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 \)

which for equal elastic constants becomes the familiar \(2\mu\,\frac{1+\nu}{1-\nu}\,\delta^2 V\). It grows with the volume, so a large precipitate with a large misfit can lower its energy by losing coherency and forming misfit dislocations at the interface, and the tool warns when the product \(|\delta|\,r_0\) is large. The energy does not include the interaction of the precipitate with the applied stress.

Limits of the model

  • The matrix and the precipitate are isotropic and linear elastic. Real crystals are anisotropic, which changes the shape of the field, though not its \(r^{-3}\) decay.
  • There is a single particle in an infinite matrix, so the results hold for a dilute dispersion. At a high volume fraction the fields overlap.
  • At atomic size (below about 0.5 nm) continuum elasticity holds only approximately.
  • A pore cannot carry a misfit, and the tool gives the pore field for any misfit.
Glossary
Lattice misfit, δ
The relative difference of the lattice parameters of the precipitate and the matrix, (ap − am) / am.
Coherent interface
An interface across which the lattice planes are continuous. The misfit is then taken up by elastic strain.
Eigenstrain
A strain that a region would have in the absence of the constraint of its surroundings, such as the misfit strain of a precipitate.
Equivalent inclusion
Eshelby’s replacement of a particle with different elastic constants by a matrix-like particle that carries a fictitious eigenstrain.
Constrained strain, ε
The part of the misfit that actually appears as strain in the inclusion once the matrix resists it.
Mean (hydrostatic) stress
One third of the trace of the stress tensor. A positive value is tension.
Von Mises stress
A scalar measure of the shear (deviatoric) part of the stress, used for yielding.
Disturbance field
The stress minus the applied stress: what the precipitate adds.
Stress concentration factor
The ratio of the largest local stress to the applied stress.
Bulk modulus, K
The resistance to uniform volume change, K = 2μ(1 + ν)/[3(1 − 2ν)] for an isotropic solid.
