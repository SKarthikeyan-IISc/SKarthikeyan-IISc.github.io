Passive Rotation Matrix and Tensor Transformation

Find the matrix a that takes components from one coordinate system to another, from crystallographic directions or from axis–angle rotations, and use it to transform tensors of rank 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Instructions for use
  • Lattice: choose a crystal system, or edit a, b, c, α, β, γ. Directions are entered as three-index [uvw] in the basis of the lattice vectors. For a cubic crystal this is the same as Cartesian components along the cube edges.
  • Initial coordinate system: for each axis x, y, z, enter the crystallographic direction that is parallel to it. The three directions must be mutually perpendicular and form a right-handed set; tick complete z from x × y to have the third axis found for you.
  • Final coordinate system: choose how it is specified. (a) Crystallographic directions: enter the directions parallel to x′, y′, z′. (b) Axis–angle rotations: enter one or more rotations, each an axis and an angle in degrees, applied in the order listed. The coordinate system is turned by the angle, counter-clockwise (right-hand rule) when looking from the tip of the axis towards the origin. The axis can be given as a crystal direction [uvw], as components along the initial axes (both fixed in space), or as components along the current, rotating axes.
  • Results: the passive rotation matrix a (each row is a new axis written in the old axes), checks that it is a proper rotation, the equivalent single rotation, the new axes as crystal directions, and a drawing of the old (blue) and new (orange) axes against the unit cell. Drag the drawing to turn the view.
  • Tensors: choose the rank, load an example or type the components in the initial system; the components in the new system are computed at once. The norm of the tensor is unchanged by a rotation, which gives a quick check. For a rank-4 tensor the 6×6 Voigt form can also be shown.

Physics of the problem

Passive rotation and the matrix a

A passive rotation turns the coordinate system and leaves the physical object (a vector, a stress, a crystal) where it is. Only the numbers used to describe it change. If the unit vectors of the new axes are \(\mathbf{e}'_i\) and those of the old axes are \(\mathbf{e}_j\), the matrix of direction cosines

\( a_{ij} = \mathbf{e}'_i \cdot \mathbf{e}_j = \cos\angle(x'_i, x_j) \)

carries the components from the old to the new system. Row \(i\) of \(a\) is the new axis \(x'_i\) written in the old axes, and column \(j\) is the old axis \(x_j\) written in the new axes. Because both sets of axes are orthonormal and right-handed, \(a\) is a proper rotation:

\( a\,a^{T} = I, \qquad \det a = +1, \qquad a^{-1} = a^{T} \)

An active rotation matrix \(R\), which turns the object by the same angle, is the transpose of the passive one: \(a = R^{T}\). Using the wrong one of the two gives the inverse rotation.

From crystallographic directions

A direction \([uvw]\) is the vector \(u\mathbf{a}+v\mathbf{b}+w\mathbf{c}\) in the lattice basis. Writing each direction in a common Cartesian frame fixed to the crystal (here \(\mathbf{a}\parallel X\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) in the \(XY\) plane) and normalising it gives the rows of two matrices: \(E_i\) for the initial axes and \(E_f\) for the final axes. A vector with components \(v\) in the initial system has components \(E_f E_i^{T} v\) in the final one, so

\( a = E_f\,E_i^{T} \)

If the initial axes are the cube edges, \(E_i=I\) and the rows of \(a\) are simply the normalised new directions. For example, \(x'\parallel[1\bar{1}0]\), \(y'\parallel[11\bar{2}]\), \(z'\parallel[111]\) gives rows \((1,-1,0)/\sqrt2\), \((1,1,-2)/\sqrt6\) and \((1,1,1)/\sqrt3\). In a non-cubic lattice the directions \([uvw]\) are generally not perpendicular even when the indices look orthogonal, and the tool checks this with the lattice metric. The direction normal to the plane \((hkl)\) equals \([hkl]\) only in a cubic crystal.

From axis–angle rotations

Turning the coordinate system by \(\theta\) about the unit axis \(\mathbf{n}\) (counter-clockwise looking from the tip of \(\mathbf{n}\)) gives the passive matrix

\( a_{ij} = \cos\theta\,\delta_{ij} + (1-\cos\theta)\,n_i n_j + \sin\theta\,\varepsilon_{ijk} n_k \)

For a rotation about \(z\) this is \(a=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0\\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\). With several rotations the tool follows the axes themselves: each rotation turns the three axes about a fixed physical line, so the matrix of the whole sequence is \(a = E_f E_i^{T}\) with the final axes found after the last step. When all axes are fixed in space, the matrix of a sequence of rotations is \(a = a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n\). When each axis is given in the current, rotating system, it is \(a = a_n \cdots a_2 a_1\). The order matters, because rotations do not commute. Any sequence is equivalent to a single rotation, with \(\cos\theta=(\operatorname{tr}a-1)/2\) and the axis given by the antisymmetric part of \(a\).

Transformation of tensors

The same matrix transforms the components of a tensor of any rank, with one factor of \(a\) for each index (the summation convention is used):

\( v'_i = a_{ij}\,v_j \)
\( T'_{ij} = a_{ik}\,a_{jl}\,T_{kl} \)
\( T'_{ijk} = a_{il}\,a_{jm}\,a_{kn}\,T_{lmn} \)
\( T'_{ijkl} = a_{ip}\,a_{jq}\,a_{kr}\,a_{ls}\,T_{pqrs} \)

The tensors have \(3\), \(9\), \(27\) and \(81\) components, which symmetry reduces: a symmetric second-rank tensor (stress, strain, conductivity) has 6 independent components, the elastic stiffness \(C_{ijkl}\) has 21 in the general case and 3 in a cubic crystal. Examples are rank 1: force, polarisation; rank 2: stress, strain, thermal conductivity; rank 3: the piezoelectric tensor \(d_{ijk}\); rank 4: the elastic stiffness and compliance. The inverse transformation uses \(a^{T}\) in place of \(a\).

Because \(a\) is orthogonal, quantities built by full contraction do not change, for example the trace of a second-rank tensor and the norm \(\sqrt{T_{ij\ldots}T_{ij\ldots}}\) of a tensor of any rank. When a tensor is carried into a coordinate system that is a symmetry operation of the crystal, its components do not change. This is a useful test: the cubic stiffness is unchanged by a turn of 90° about a cube edge, and the piezoelectric tensor of quartz (point group 32) by a turn of 120° about \(z\) and of 180° about \(x\).

The Voigt form writes a tensor that is symmetric in \(ij\) and in \(kl\) as a \(6\times6\) matrix, with the index pairs \(11,22,33,23,13,12\) written as \(1,\ldots,6\). It is only a way to display the components: it does not transform by multiplication with \(a\), and the transformation is always done on the full tensor.

Glossary
Passive rotation
A turn of the coordinate system with the physical object left fixed. Only its components change.
Active rotation
A turn of the object with the coordinate system left fixed. Its matrix is the transpose of the passive matrix for the same angle.
Direction cosines
The cosines of the angles between the new and old axes. They are the elements aij of the passive rotation matrix.
Proper rotation
An orthogonal matrix with determinant +1, which keeps the handedness of the axes.
Axis–angle pair
A rotation described by a unit vector (the axis) and an angle about it, taken as positive in the right-hand sense.
Tensor rank
The number of indices, and so of factors of a in the transformation law.
Voigt notation
A compact 6×6 way to write a fourth-rank tensor that is symmetric in its first and in its last pair of indices.
[uvw]
A crystal direction, written in the basis of the lattice vectors.
