Stereographic Projection of a Cubic Crystal

How the directions and plane normals of a cubic crystal are mapped from a sphere onto a flat circle, and how the pattern changes as the crystal is rotated.

Instructions for use
  • Rotate the crystal: click and drag inside the 3D Crystal View to turn the unit cell in real time. The projection on the right updates as you drag.
  • Orient the centre: type a direction [u v w] (for example 1 1 1) and press Orient Center to bring that direction to the middle of the projection.
  • Plot a pole: type Miller indices [h k l] (for example 1 2 3), press Plot Pole, and tick Custom to show it in orange.
  • Pole families: the check boxes under the 3D view switch the <001> (red), <011> (green) and <111> (blue) families, and the custom pole, on and off in both views. Tick at least one to see poles.
  • Display options: show the Wulff net, show the Miller-index labels, and include the southern hemisphere (open circles).

Physics of the problem

The orientation of a crystal is described by the directions of its crystallographic axes, planes and directions relative to the sample. A direction (or the normal to a plane) is a point on the surface of a sphere that surrounds the crystal, the pole. A stereographic projection draws that sphere on a flat circle: a line is drawn from a pole on the upper hemisphere to the south pole of the sphere, and the point where it cuts the equatorial plane is the projection. For a unit vector \((x, y, z)\) with \(z\ge 0\):

\( X = \dfrac{x}{1+z}, \qquad Y = \dfrac{y}{1+z} \)

Poles in the lower hemisphere are projected from the north pole instead, and are drawn as open circles so that they can be told apart. The projection preserves angles: a circle on the sphere is a circle on the projection. The angle between two poles can therefore be measured along a great circle with the Wulff net, a grid of the projected meridians and parallels of the sphere, spaced at 10°.

For a cubic crystal, the angle \(\phi\) between two directions \([h_1k_1l_1]\) and \([h_2k_2l_2]\) is given by the dot product, which is also the angle between the plane normals with the same indices:

\( \cos\phi = \dfrac{h_1h_2 + k_1k_2 + l_1l_2}{\sqrt{h_1^2+k_1^2+l_1^2}\,\sqrt{h_2^2+k_2^2+l_2^2}} \)

With [001] at the centre, the first quadrant of the projection is the standard triangle bounded by [001], [011] and [111]. Because of the cubic symmetry, every orientation can be brought into this triangle, so it is the area used for inverse pole figures. The angles between its corners are 45° ([001] to [011]), 54.74° ([001] to [111]) and 35.26° ([011] to [111]). The <001>, <011> and <111> families have 6, 12 and 8 poles respectively (counting both signs).

Stereographic projections are used to represent the orientation of crystals and the texture of polycrystals, to find the slip systems that are active in a given loading direction, and to index diffraction patterns.

Glossary
Pole
The point where a crystal direction, or the normal to a crystal plane, meets the reference sphere.
Miller indices [hkl]
Integers that denote a direction (or the normal to the plane (hkl)) in a cubic crystal.
<hkl> family
All directions that are equivalent by the symmetry of the crystal.
Stereographic projection
An angle-preserving map of the sphere onto a plane.
Wulff net
A stereographic projection of the meridians and parallels of a sphere, used to measure angles.
Primitive circle
The outer circle of the projection, which is the equator of the sphere.
Southern hemisphere
Poles pointing into the lower half of the sphere, drawn as open circles.
