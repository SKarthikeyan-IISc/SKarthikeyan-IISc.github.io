Homogeneous Deformation of a 2D Square Lattice

How a displacement gradient matrix moves every point of a lattice, and how it splits into stretch, shear and rotation.

Instructions for use
  • Sliders e11, e12, e21, e22: the four components of the displacement gradient matrix, each from −1 to 1. The black lattice is the deformed lattice; the grey lattice is the original.
  • Presets: load a simple stretch, a shear or a rotation, then change a slider to see what each component does on its own.
  • Points per side, point size: change the number of lattice points and the size of the dots.
  • Show original, show deformed: switch either lattice on or off.
  • Reset sliders returns the lattice to its original shape.
  • The panel on the right gives the strain and rotation calculated from the four numbers.

Physics of the problem

In a homogeneous deformation every point of a body is displaced by an amount that is a linear function of its position. A point at position \(\mathbf{X}\) moves to \(\mathbf{x}\):

\( \begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} e_{11} & e_{12}\\ e_{21} & e_{22} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} X\\ Y \end{pmatrix} \)

The matrix \(e_{ij}=\partial u_i/\partial X_j\) is the displacement gradient. Straight lines stay straight and parallel lines stay parallel, so a lattice remains a lattice. This is the case in the elastic deformation of a single crystal, and is the starting point of the idea of affine deformation of a solid, in which the atoms follow the macroscopic deformation.

Any displacement gradient is the sum of a symmetric part and an antisymmetric part:

\( e_{ij} = \varepsilon_{ij} + \omega_{ij},\qquad \varepsilon_{ij}=\tfrac12\,(e_{ij}+e_{ji}),\quad \omega_{ij}=\tfrac12\,(e_{ij}-e_{ji}) \)

The symmetric part \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) is the strain: its diagonal terms stretch or compress the lattice and its off-diagonal terms shear it, changing the lengths of lattice vectors and the angles between them. The antisymmetric part is a rigid rotation through an angle \(\omega = \tfrac12(e_{21}-e_{12})\) and changes no lengths. The two can be seen in the presets: "Shear X" and "Shear Y" each contain half a pure shear and half a rotation, whereas "Shear X and Y" (\(e_{12}=e_{21}\)) is a pure shear, and "Rotate" (\(e_{12}=-e_{21}\)) is a pure rotation.

The decomposition is exact only for small displacement gradients. For a large \(e\), such as the rotation here with a value of 0.2 or more, the lattice also grows slightly, because a finite rotation is not the same as \(I+e\) with an antisymmetric \(e\); the change in area, \(\det(I+e)\), shows this effect. This is the reason for using the finite-strain measures in a large deformation.

Glossary
Displacement gradient, eij
The derivative of the displacement of a point with respect to its position.
Strain, εij
The symmetric part of the displacement gradient: stretch and shear.
Rotation, ω
The antisymmetric part: a rigid rotation of the lattice.
Homogeneous deformation
A deformation in which the displacement gradient is the same everywhere.
Pure shear
A shear strain without rotation.
Simple shear
A shear that is the sum of a pure shear and a rotation.
